# Founder Story

*This is the origin story — the specific trip that surfaced the problem Lamyig exists to solve. For what Lamyig is today, see [`00-executive-summary.md`](00-executive-summary.md) and [`06-product-philosophy.md`](06-product-philosophy.md).*

In 2026, after leaving my job, I set out on a solo motorcycle journey through Spiti with a simple plan: travel slowly, meet local people, and keep my accommodation costs as low as possible.

Instead of booking hotels in advance, I followed a routine. Whenever I reached a village, I would stop at a small shop or a tea stall, park the motorcycle, and ask whoever was around whether anyone in the village hosted travellers.

## What the online travel economy looks like from a motorcycle

During peak season, towns like Kalpa and Reckong Peo were dominated by expensive online listings. Rooms cost between ₹3,000 and ₹5,000 per night — far beyond what made sense for a solo budget traveller. Negotiation was uncommon, and most visible accommodation catered to tourists who had booked online before arriving.

But the moment I moved away from these commercial hubs, a different pattern appeared.

In Shego, a small village before Kaza, I stopped only to rest. A local uncle noticed me sitting outside and asked if I needed a place to stay. His family welcomed me into their home, gave me breakfast and dinner, offered tea throughout the day, and treated me like family — for ₹800.

The same thing happened in Losar, in Gue, and in Shichling near Dhankar. In each place I found family-run homestays that were affordable, authentic, and deeply welcoming. Many charged a fraction of what was listed online, and included homemade meals and genuine hospitality.

This was not unique to Spiti. A year earlier, riding through Zanskar, I had pre-booked a tent near Gonbo Rangjon for ₹1,500 per person. After arriving, I discovered a homestay in nearby Kargyak village offering a room and meals for ₹500 — and a far richer local experience.

Every one of these discoveries happened by chance.

None of these homestays appeared where travellers normally search. Most had no website. Some were not on Google Maps. Almost none were on booking platforms. In several villages, internet connectivity itself was unreliable, so digital participation was not even an option. The people running these homes were not trying to maximise revenue — they simply had no way to participate in the online travel economy.

The result: travellers pay more than they need to, while local families with spare rooms remain invisible.

## The puncture

Accommodation turned out to be the smaller half of the problem.

On the way to Reckong Peo, I got a puncture in the middle of nowhere. There was no signboard, no listing, no way to know where the nearest repair was. I rode back five kilometres on a flat tyre, hoping the tube would hold.

That is when it became clear: on a trip like this, you need a room maybe once a day. But you need *information* twenty times a day. Where is fuel? Is there a mechanic? Does BSNL work here? Can I refill water? Is food available after 8 PM? Is the road ahead broken? Can I pitch a tent?

Google Maps doesn't answer these questions for places like Baatal — a spot near the Chacha-Chachi dhaba with no network, no nearby village, nothing. Booking platforms don't answer them. Instagram doesn't answer them.

People answer them. The tea stall answers them. The last rider who passed through answers them.

## The realisation

The problem is not a shortage of places to stay, or of mechanics, or of water points. The problem is that the most useful knowledge about remote India is undocumented — it exists only in local memory and word of mouth, and it disappears the moment you ride out of earshot.

Commercial platforms have little incentive to fix this. There is no commission to earn on a water refill point or a puncture repair in Shichling.

A community can fix it. That is what Lamyig is for.
